# Community impact and validation plan

## Problem addressed

Communication between Deaf or hard-of-hearing people who use Indian Sign Language and hearing people who do not understand ISL can be difficult in everyday situations. Access to trustworthy learning resources and opportunities to build basic vocabulary can help reduce this gap. A camera-based word-recognition prototype may also demonstrate how computer vision can support accessibility research, provided its limitations are made clear.

## Intended beneficiaries

- Deaf and hard-of-hearing people who use Indian Sign Language.
- Parents, classmates, teachers, service staff and community members who want to learn basic ISL vocabulary.
- Student volunteers and educators who want a simple way to demonstrate official learning resources.

The project must be co-reviewed with ISL users/educators. Hearing students alone cannot validate whether signs, grammar, regional forms or communication outcomes are correct.

## What this prototype provides today

- A clear entry point to the official ISLRTC dictionary and related resources.
- A beginner vocabulary recall activity.
- A phrase lookup shortcut that directs each word to the alphabetical list in the official dictionary.
- A browser-based camera input and model-service integration shell.
- A sentence builder and local saved-message history.
- A local feedback form.

The vocabulary quiz is not sign-language fluency testing. The camera interface is not a working AI translator until a valid model inference service is connected and evaluated.

## Suggested community activity

1. Contact a local Deaf community group, ISL instructor, special educator or accessibility organisation before arranging a session.
2. Explain the project purpose and its limitations in advance.
3. Ask participants whether they are comfortable participating; participation must be optional.
4. Do not capture, retain or publish faces, hands, voices or identifying details without specific informed consent.
5. Demonstrate the official dictionary and learning/practice pages first.
6. Demonstrate the camera screen only as a prototype. If the model is not connected, say so explicitly and use the clearly marked sample preview only to demonstrate the sentence-builder interface.
7. Ask participants to describe what is understandable, confusing or missing. Provide a way to give feedback in their preferred communication mode.
8. Record only anonymised observations and consented feedback.
9. Implement at least one improvement based on feedback and show what changed.

## Example evaluation questions

- Was it easy to find a trusted ISL video for a word?
- Was the difference between a vocabulary practice quiz and actual sign recognition clear?
- Were text size, contrast and navigation comfortable?
- Did the project explain camera use and data handling clearly?
- What should be changed before this prototype is used in a real service setting?

## Evidence to collect honestly

- Date and general setting of the activity.
- Number of participants only when they consent to anonymous counting.
- Roles/categories of participants only when appropriate and consented.
- Anonymised feedback themes.
- Improvements made based on feedback.
- Screenshots of the application with no personal data.
- Model test results only from a real labelled dataset and actual model predictions.

Never invent interviews, community partners, user numbers, testimonials, recognition results or social-impact statistics.

## Outcome indicators

Possible indicators include number of official learning resources accessed during a session, completion rate of the vocabulary practice activity, participant-reported clarity, accessibility issues found and number of improvements implemented. These measure project engagement and usability, not the project's effect on broader disability inclusion.
